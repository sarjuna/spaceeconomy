Five minutes, three questions. The space economy is heading toward 1.8 trillion dollars. My research asks three things about it. First, is the demand real, or are investors chasing noise? Second, as the sky fills with satellites, who protects it? And third, can we actually build satellites differently, using what that research tells us? I’ll take one slide on each.

The first strand starts with a simple problem: investors and policymakers in the space economy struggle to tell real demand from hype. So I’m exploring whether what people say — public attention, online sentiment, stock prices and policy debate — can be mapped onto what the industry actually builds: launches, satellites and capital. This is the working dashboard, built entirely on open, public data for India and the United States; the chart shows US public and NewSpace attention over a decade. The interesting questions are whether sentiment leads real build-out or just follows the headlines, which signals can genuinely be trusted, and how the answers differ between the two countries.

03

Now what happens when that demand turns into hardware. This animation plays out the live FCC filings over five years. On the left is visible light: satellites shine where sunlight catches them, the background brightens and the stars fade. On the right is infrared: orbital data centres have to dump the heat from their computers through radiators, and that glow continues all night, even in Earth’s shadow. On these assumptions we pass the roughly 100,000-satellite ceiling that ESO’s study says astronomy can tolerate as early as 2028. That opens four questions no regulator can yet answer. Who sets a cap, when 1.7 million satellites are proposed? Who writes an infrared standard that doesn’t exist? Who pays for lost telescope time, ozone loss and debris — harms no licence prices today? And when one country licenses, but everyone shares the sky, who decides? That is the policy agenda of my brief, Crowded Orbits, Fading Skies.

04

If AI compute moves into orbit, it raises genuinely new research problems. Three I’m working on. First: where should a model live when every node is moving? We form clusters of satellites by latency and free GPU space, and split the model across them. On our ground testbed that placement decodes 2.34 times faster than an unoptimised in-space deployment, and 1.33 times faster than terrestrial. Second — and this is what the animation shows — how does a conversation survive a handover? Satellites cross the sky in minutes, so the pod serving a terminal sets while the next one rises. The conversation’s memory, the KV cache, has to follow. We can relay it across the laser link, about 1.2 seconds on a 10 gigabit link, or replay the prompt on the new pod, about 2 seconds. The rule: relay when links beat about 2.9 gigabits per second, otherwise replay. Third: how does a satellite decide on its own, out of ground contact? A small fine-tuned language model decides whether to run, queue, hand off or drop each task, with about 87 percent accuracy, beating a rule engine. The impact: fewer satellites doing more.

05

So how does it come together? Maargin is where these strands meet, and it rests on two principles. Frugal by design: small satellites under 500 kilograms on ride-share launches, software-defined radios instead of fixed hardware, our own optical links that are 90 percent cheaper and 40 to 50 percent lighter, Indian manufacturing with off-the-shelf parts, and onboard AI that processes data in orbit, so we transmit only what matters and cut data transmission costs. Together that cuts capital costs by at least 87 percent compared with a mega-constellation: the full network costs 400 to 480 million dollars. And responsible by default: 324 satellites for planet-scale coverage, not a million. Our communications shells sit at 1,000 to 1,200 kilometres, so each satellite appears three to five times fainter overhead than at 550. We also keep them dark: low-reflectivity coatings, and solar arrays turned edge-on to the ground during the twilight hours when satellites are most visible. The impact: planet-scale broadband and AI compute for under half a billion dollars.
